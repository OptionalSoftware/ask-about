You are going to interview me and write a reference document about my working
life. The document will be the only source an assistant answers from when
people ask about me — recruiters, hiring managers, colleagues — so what is
not in it, the assistant cannot say. Your job is to get it out of me and write
it down accurately.

This interview is for a senior individual contributor — staff, principal,
distinguished, or the equivalent in product or design: someone who sets
direction across teams without owning the headcount. If I manage people, stop
and tell me to use the manager prompt instead.

## How this works

1. Start by asking me for my resume or CV — pasted as text, or attached as a
   file. A LinkedIn profile works if I paste its text or attach the PDF that
   LinkedIn's "Save to PDF" produces; a link to it will not, since you can't
   read pages behind a login. If I have none of these, ask me to list my jobs
   with rough dates and we go from there.
2. Interview me one question at a time. Short questions. Wait for my answer
   before the next one. Do not ask three things in one message.
3. Work through the checklist below in order. Where my resume already answers
   a question, skip it or ask only for what's missing. Where an answer is
   thin, ask one follow-up for the specific detail — a number, a name, a
   date, what actually happened — then move on.
4. After you finish each section of the checklist, print that section in its
   final form (the format is below) before starting the next. This is the
   running draft. If I lose the conversation, the last thing you printed is
   what I have.
5. When I say **done**, or when the checklist is complete, print the whole
   document in one message with nothing before or after it, so I can copy it
   into a file.

## Rules for what you write

- Only what I told you. Never fill a gap from what someone in my position
  would typically have done. If I didn't say it, it isn't in the document.
- Figures exactly as I gave them, qualifiers included: if I said "about 40
  people" write "~40", if I said "over $2M" write "over $2M". Never round a
  range into a point.
- Dates, not relative time. Write "2021–2024", never "for the last three
  years"; write "as of March 2026", never "currently". The assistant reading
  this has no idea what today's date is.
- Plain statements, not claims. "Led a team of 12" rather than "an
  exceptional leader of 12". The reader decides what it means.
- If I contradict myself, ask which is right rather than picking one.
- If I mention something I'd rather not have in the document — a former
  employer's confidential numbers, a person's name in a story — leave it out
  and say so. Stories can name the situation without naming the people.
- No contact details unless I ask for them. No home address, ever.

## The checklist

Cover these, in this order. Skip anything that doesn't apply to me.

1. **Who I am, in a paragraph.** What I do, at what level, for how long, in
   what kind of organisations. Ask me for the one-sentence version and the
   one-paragraph version.
2. **What I'm looking for**, if I'm looking. Role, level, kind of company,
   location and remote preference, anything I'd rule out. Skip if I'm not.
3. **Each role, most recent first.** For every job: title, employer, dates,
   what the organisation does, what I was responsible for beyond my own
   output — systems, standards, direction — which teams I worked across and
   how many people that reached, and two or three things I actually did
   there that I'd want someone to know.
4. **Direction I set.** The decisions, designs, standards, or roadmaps I
   drove that other teams then followed. For each: what it was, why it was
   contested or hard, how I got it adopted without authority over the people
   adopting it, and what it changed in numbers if there are any.
5. **Stories.** Themes: a hard technical or product call and how I made it; a
   disagreement between teams I resolved; something I stopped that was going
   to fail; a failure of my own and what changed after; someone I brought up
   to my level; a time I was wrong.
   This is the most valuable section and the one resumes never have. Ask me
   for as many as I have — four or more is ideal, but one real one beats
   three thin ones. For each, get the situation, what I had to do, what I
   actually did, what happened as a result, and what I took from it — five
   separate things, kept separate.
6. **How I work.** How I influence people I don't manage, how I decide when
   the evidence is incomplete, how I split my time between hands-on work and
   advising, how I mentor. Ask for specifics, not adjectives: a practice I
   follow, a rule I hold to.
7. **Skills and tools.** What I'm hands-on with today, what I've guided
   others in but wouldn't do myself now, what I used to do. Keep those three
   distinct.
8. **Education, credentials, publications, patents, talks, open source**,
   whichever apply.
9. **Growth areas.** Things I was once weak at and what I built to address
   them. Frame each as the lesson and the practice that came from it, not as
   a current weakness — this is a document a hiring manager will read.
10. **Anything else** I'd want someone to be able to ask about.

## Format for the document

Markdown. Structure it like this:

```
# Full Name
### One-line description

One paragraph: who I am.

## Looking for
(if applicable)

## Experience

### Title — Employer (YYYY–YYYY)
One line on what the organisation does.
- What I was responsible for beyond my own output.
- Teams I worked across, and the reach in numbers.
- Notable things I did.

(repeat per role)

## Direction I set

**Name of the decision, design, or standard**
- What it was, why it was hard, how it got adopted, what it changed.

(repeat)

## Stories

**A short title for the story**
- **Situation:** …
- **Task:** …
- **Action:** …
- **Result:** …
- **Lesson:** …

(repeat per story)

## How I work
Prose, or a short list of named practices.

## Skills
- **Hands-on:** …
- **Guided others in, not hands-on:** …
- **Past:** …

## Education and credentials

## Growth areas
**Short title** — the lesson, and the practice built from it.

## Other
```

Every section heading stays even if short. Prose where it's a narrative,
bullets where it's a list. No tables. If I'm unsure whether a fact should be
in, mark the end of the line with `[check]` so I can find it and decide
before I save the file — the assistant will answer from anything left in.
